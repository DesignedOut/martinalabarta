ABOUTI look at how the look and feel of a product — its form, materials, colour — can carry assumptions and discriminatory meanings about gender and intersectional identities more broadly, that go unquestioned, until they become the norm: the default, archetypal form that quietly alienates anyone who doesn't fit in. My work sits at the intersection of design aesthetics, product language, and social justice, with a focus on gender representation and what I call sensitive design. Since design is never neutral, I try to pass that same critical lens on pedagogically too, going beyond exploring it only in my own research.

02 — PhD Research
Destabilising Archetypes through Product Language: Towards a Sensitive Design Compass
Institution Politecnico di Milano Department Design Since 2024 Expected March 2028
Design Processes Design Aesthetics Product Design Language Research through Design Design Justice
Abstract

This research investigates the role of Product Language — how products communicate meaning through their material, formal, and aesthetic qualities — in shaping and reinforcing stereotypical and stigmatising social representations within industrial product design. Starting from the premise that design is not neutral, the study examines how visual and formal conventions stabilise into design archetypes that may perpetuate discriminatory norms related to gender, race, age, or ability. Positioned at the intersection of Product Language studies and critical design approaches concerned with social justice and inclusivity, the research aims to develop a conceptual framework and practical orientation for designers to navigate Product Language choices more critically, sensitively, and inclusively.

Workshop Interclasse — "Seeing Beyond: Visual Language of Inclusive Products" Leader
Università degli Studi di Sassari, Alghero, Italy
The workshop invited participants to reflect on how the design of many products is shaped by the biases and perspectives of both designers and users — aspects that manifest in visual language. Certain design choices send signals that lead some users to feel recognised and welcomed, while others contribute to dynamics of discrimination or marginalisation. The goal was to learn to identify these mechanisms through analysis and design experimentation, developing concrete proposals that rethought the visual language of products in a more conscious, sensitive, and responsible way.

Participants: 1st–3rd year BSc Design students.
Role: Workshop leader — prepared agenda, activities, and facilitated development.
